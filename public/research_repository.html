The shape of the problem

Speech is the densest information channel humans possess. In ordinary conversation, we encode roughly two hundred words per minute through nothing more than coordinated contractions of muscles in the throat, jaw, tongue, and face. Decades of research have established that the electrical and mechanical signatures of those contractions persist even when no sound is produced, whether the person silently mouths a sentence, internally rehearses one, or simply intends to speak. The premise of Sub-vocal Recognition is that this residual signal, properly captured and decoded, is rich enough to reconstruct what someone meant to say. The work summarized below traces how that premise has been tested, refined, and pushed toward something practical over the past four decades.

The earliest attempts, in the 1980s, used surface electromyography to recognize a handful of vowel sounds.[13] They worked, in the limited sense that classifiers could distinguish 'ah' from 'ee' above chance, but the gap from phonetic curiosity to usable interface seemed enormous. Progress since then has come slowly, as a long accumulation of incremental answers to a stack of nested questions: where on the body should sensors go, what signal modality is best, how should the signal be featurized, what model architecture decodes it, how do you handle variability between people and across days, and how do you fit any of this into a device someone would actually wear.

Foundations in surface electromyography

The most influential body of foundational work comes from Meltzner, Heaton, Kline, and their collaborators, whose decade-long program centered on a system called MUTE, the Mouthed-speech Understanding and Transcription Engine. Their 2018 paper in the Journal of Neural Engineering reported a recognition system trained on a 2,200-word vocabulary across more than 1,200 continuous phrases, using eleven surface electromyography sensors arrayed across the face and neck.[1] Across that 2,200-word vocabulary, word error rates averaged 8.9 percent, and dropped as low as 1.4 percent for the best-performing speaker. The architecture is now standard reference material for the field: signals from each sensor are bandpass-filtered and segmented by a custom speech-activity detector, parameterized using Mel-frequency cepstral coefficients adapted to the lower frequency content of muscle signals, dimensionally reduced through heteroscedastic linear discriminant analysis, and decoded by a triphone Hidden Markov Model with subspace Gaussian mixtures.[1] The takeaway was that techniques developed for acoustic speech recognition could be ported almost directly to muscle signals, provided the front-end was tuned for the physiology of EMG rather than the acoustics of air.

The same group then asked the question that mattered most clinically: would the system still work for people who could no longer vocalize? Their companion 2018 study recorded eight men who had undergone total laryngectomy at least six months prior.[2] Despite the surgical removal of the larynx and the loss of any voiced reference signal, the system achieved a 10.3 percent word error rate using all eight sensors and 13.6 percent using a reduced four-sensor configuration, both evaluated on a 2,500-word vocabulary. This was a meaningful result for two reasons. It established that the articulatory musculature of the face and tongue remains intact and informative after laryngectomy, and it showed that the algorithm could be trained without ever hearing the speaker's voice, closing a loop that earlier prosthetic-voice work had required acoustic data to complete.[2]

Vojtech and colleagues at Delsys took on a different question: could the same EMG signal also tell you how something was said?[3] Working with four typical speakers and four laryngectomees, they used eight sEMG sensors to recognize lexical content and to classify whether the first word, last word, or no word in a phrase received stress. Across phrases drawn from a balanced eight-word stress-position corpus, they reported 96.3 percent word accuracy and 91.2 percent accuracy on the three-class stress location task. When the recognized words and stress patterns were then synthesized into personalized voices, naive listeners rated the synthetic output as significantly more intelligible and acceptable than electrolarynx alternatives, and could correctly identify which word the speaker meant to stress far more often.[3] For laryngectomees, who currently rely on devices that produce a flat, mechanical sound, the prospect of a synthetic voice that conveys phrase-level emphasis is more than a technical novelty.

Moving away from the face

The Meltzner work, for all its accuracy, lived with a practical ceiling. Sensors on the face and neck require adhesives, conductive gels, and careful placement; users described the experience as visually conspicuous and skin-irritating after long sessions. A more recent line of research has therefore tried to move sensing off the face entirely, into form factors people might already be willing to wear. Wu and colleagues at UC Berkeley demonstrated that a wireless neckband holding ten dry, gold-plated, 3D-printed electrodes, paired with a coin-sized recording module worn behind the head, could classify an 11-word vocabulary of minimal pairs at 92.7 percent accuracy across two speakers.[4] Ablation experiments showed that the neck-only signal was nearly as informative as one combining neck and face electrodes, and that the device could capture EMG features that linearly mapped to self-supervised acoustic representations from the WavLM model.[4] This last result is conceptually important: it suggests that the neck signal carries enough acoustic information about speech to be mapped onto continuous sound representations, well beyond what a binary on/off picture of muscle activity would provide.

An ETH Zurich group led by Meier, Cossettini, and Benini took the engineering side of the same idea further.[5] Their textile neckband, built around the BioGAP-Ultra platform (a parallel ultra-low-power processor running at 22.2 milliwatts), integrates fourteen fully-dry, fully-differential channels into an adjustable fabric band. On an eight-word command set, they reported 87 percent accuracy for vocalized speech and 68 percent for silent articulation under five-fold cross-validation. More importantly, they did something most studies in the field have avoided: they removed and repositioned the neckband between sessions, then evaluated leave-one-session-out performance on the same eight-word vocabulary to simulate everyday wear. Accuracy dropped to 64 percent for vocalized and 54 percent for silent speech.[5] The drop measures the gap between the laboratory and daily life. Any deployable SSR system will have to close that gap.

The choice of which muscles to record from has, until recently, been treated as a matter of inheritance: each new study tends to use whatever electrode placement worked for someone before. A pilot study by Salomons and colleagues at the HiTZ Basque Center attacked this question directly for Spanish, recording from fourteen candidate muscles in a single speaker and ranking each by per-channel phone-classification accuracy.[6] They settled on an asymmetric eight-channel configuration targeting the digastric, depressor anguli oris, risorius, levator labii superioris, masseter, zygomaticus major, depressor labii superioris, and stylohyoid: five facial muscles and three neck muscles. Two of these (the depressor labii superioris and stylohyoid) had not appeared in prior literature, suggesting that the conventional electrode maps inherited from the early 2000s may have been leaving useful signal on the table.[6] The work also serves as a reminder that small-muscle EMG is sensitive to placement: the orbicularis oris channels in their experiments suffered repeatedly from sweat, condensation, and detachment caused by the curvature of the lips themselves.

The shift from custom decoders to language priors

For most of its history, SSR has been a problem of training a bespoke classifier on a person's data and hoping it generalizes. Two recent papers suggest a different framing. Mohapatra and colleagues at Northwestern asked whether a frozen large language model (Llama2 or Llama3, never modified) could be taught to read unvoiced EMG by way of a small adapter network that projects EMG features into the LLM's input embedding space.[7] With only six minutes of unvoiced training data and no audible reference signal, their approach achieved a word error rate of 0.49 on a closed-vocabulary EMG-to-text task, outperforming a specialized transducer baseline by twenty percent on the same vocabulary.[7] The implication is that the heavy lifting of language understanding can be borrowed from models trained on text alone, and that the SSR-specific work reduces to teaching a small bridge between physiology and a frozen linguistic prior.

Satterlee and colleagues at San Diego State arrived at a similar conclusion from a different direction.[8] Their wearable system uses a single channel of EMG (under the chin, targeting the genioglossus and hyoglossus) and a single channel of EEG (above the ear, near the auditory cortex), both wireless, both low-power. A Siamese neural network trained with few-shot learning handles word-level segmentation from continuous sentence signals via a sliding window, sensor fusion combines the two modalities, and a trigram language model corrects syntactic errors in the predicted sequence. On a small vocabulary of military commands assembled into short sentences, the full pipeline reached 95.25 percent sentence-level accuracy.[8] Each component on its own (the few-shot learner, the fusion, the language model) produced only modest gains; in combination, they reached an accuracy that the underlying classifier alone could not approach. Both papers point the same way: better priors do more for SSR accuracy than additional sensing channels.

Tang and colleagues at Cambridge and Beihang University recently took this approach further with a system aimed at stroke patients suffering from dysarthria.[17] Their wearable choker holds two textile strain sensor channels, one over the throat muscles and one over the carotid artery, with screen-printed graphene that develops ordered cracks under stretching to detect the small skin vibrations produced during silent articulation. The decoding pipeline segments the signal into 144-millisecond tokens, classifies each with a 1D ResNet-18 distilled from a larger ResNet-101 (a 75 percent cut in computational cost for less than a percentage point of accuracy loss), and hands the token sequence to two GPT-4o-mini agents. The first agent reconstructs words from the token labels and corrects boundary errors through majority voting over consecutive predictions. The second agent expands the resulting sentence into a fluent, contextually appropriate utterance, drawing on the patient's emotional state (decoded separately from the carotid pulse signal) and external information like time of day and weather. Across five stroke patients tested on a 47-word vocabulary assembled into 20 sentences, the system reached a 4.2 percent word error rate and a 2.9 percent sentence error rate, and patient satisfaction with the expanded sentences was 55 percent higher than with the literal token-by-token output.[17] Two of the three working components in the pipeline (the word reconstruction and the sentence expansion) live entirely inside a frozen language model accessed via API, with the SSR-specific machinery reduced to a small classifier sitting on top of a wearable sensor.

The commercial frontier and recent reviews

The most public-facing project in the field is AlterEgo, which began as a 2018 paper from Arnav Kapur and collaborators at the MIT Media Lab.[18] The original device used seven EMG channels arranged across the lower face and neck, with the locations chosen by ranking thirty candidate sites for how well they discriminated between word labels. Signals were processed into MFCC-style features and classified by a 1D convolutional neural network. On a 10-digit vocabulary used for arithmetic interaction, the system reached 92 percent median word accuracy across ten users, with a latency of around 0.43 seconds from the end of a silent utterance to the corresponding transcription.[18] The full prototype also handled small additional vocabulary sets organized hierarchically by application: an 8-city set for world clock queries, a 20-character set for chess moves, a 4-command set for IoT control, and a 5-phrase set for human-to-human messaging. Output was returned through bone conduction headphones, creating a closed loop in which a user can query a computer and receive a response without making or hearing any sound that others can perceive. The 2018 paper already demonstrated applications spanning IoT control, calendar lookup, and bidirectional human-AI gameplay in chess and Go. AlterEgo spun off into a full company in 2025 and is currently being tested in clinical trials with patients who have ALS and multiple sclerosis, where the technology offers a meaningful alternative for individuals whose vocalization is progressively failing.[13] [14] The current prototype still requires wired connections to processing hardware, but the research-to-product trajectory it represents is the clearest signal yet that SSR is leaving the laboratory.

Two recent review articles try to take stock of where SSR stands. Chowdhury and colleagues, writing in Neural Computing and Applications in 2025, walk through the history of the field from early waveform analysis to current deep-learning approaches, and survey the available datasets, sensing modalities, and decoding architectures.[15] A more focused technical review of wearable EMG neckbands covers the engineering side: electrode materials and skin-impedance management, low-power processors like BioGAP-Ultra and WANDmini, the move from MFCC features toward wavelet-based representations, and the growing use of multimodal fusion combining EMG with EEG, IMU, PPG, and magnetometer signals.[16] Both reviews land on the same conclusion. The open question is whether systems hold up when the same person wears the device on a different day, or when a different person wears it for the first time.

Where this leaves us

Three threads run through nearly all of the work above. The first is hardware: a steady, mostly successful migration from wet electrodes glued to the face toward dry, textile-integrated, low-power, and increasingly contactless sensors that can be worn for hours without irritation. The second is modeling: a shift away from training a single bespoke decoder per speaker toward architectures that lean on language priors, few-shot adaptation, and pretrained foundation models to do work that small EMG datasets cannot. The third is evaluation: a slow recognition that cross-session and cross-user accuracy, rather than laboratory peak performance, is the metric that matters, and that systems which look excellent in controlled conditions can lose twenty or thirty percentage points when the device is taken off and put back on, even on small vocabularies.

The accumulated evidence is that sub-vocal recognition is not blocked by any single technical problem. The signals are measurable; the algorithms exist; the hardware is approaching the form factor of a piece of jewelry. What remains is integration: choosing the right modality, the right placement, the right priors, and the right calibration strategy for a system that has to work for many people, on many days, in many contexts. Amtavla's work begins from this state of the field and is oriented toward the integration question.
