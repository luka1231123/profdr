Amtavla: Sub Vocal Recognition Project

The shape of the problem

Speech is the densest information channel humans possess. In ordinary conversation, we encode roughly two hundred words per minute through nothing more than coordinated contractions of muscles in the throat, jaw, tongue, and face. Decades of research have established that the electrical and mechanical signatures of those contractions persist even when no sound is produced, whether the person silently mouths a sentence, internally rehearses one, or simply intends to speak. The premise of Sub-vocal Recognition is that this residual signal, properly captured and decoded, is rich enough to reconstruct what someone meant to say. The work summarized below traces how that premise has been tested, refined, and pushed toward something practical over the past four decades.



The earliest attempts, in the 1980s, used surface electromyography to recognize a handful of vowel sounds. They worked, in the limited sense that classifiers could distinguish 'ah' from 'ee' above chance, but the gap from phonetic curiosity to usable interface seemed enormous. Progress since then has come slowly, as a long accumulation of incremental answers to a stack of nested questions: where on the body should sensors go, what signal modality is best, how should the signal be featurized, what model architecture decodes it, how do you handle variability between people and across days, and how do you fit any of this into a device someone would actually wear.

Foundations in surface electromyography

The most influential body of foundational work comes from Meltzner, Heaton, Kline, and their collaborators, whose decade-long program centered on a system called MUTE, the Mouthed-speech Understanding and Transcription Engine. Their 2018 paper in the Journal of Neural Engineering reported a recognition system trained on a 2,200-word vocabulary across more than 1,200 continuous phrases, using eleven surface electromyography sensors arrayed across the face and neck. Across that 2,200-word vocabulary, word error rates averaged 8.9 percent, and dropped as low as 1.4 percent for the best-performing speaker. The architecture is now standard reference material for the field: signals from each sensor are bandpass-filtered and segmented by a custom speech-activity detector, parameterized using Mel-frequency cepstral coefficients adapted to the lower frequency content of muscle signals, dimensionally reduced through heteroscedastic linear discriminant analysis, and decoded by a triphone Hidden Markov Model with subspace Gaussian mixtures. The takeaway was that techniques developed for acoustic speech recognition could be ported almost directly to muscle signals, provided the front-end was tuned for the physiology of EMG rather than the acoustics of air.



The same group then asked the question that mattered most clinically: would the system still work for people who could no longer vocalize? Their companion 2018 study recorded eight men who had undergone total laryngectomy at least six months prior. Despite the surgical removal of the larynx and the loss of any voiced reference signal, the system achieved a 10.3 percent word error rate using all eight sensors and 13.6 percent using a reduced four-sensor configuration, both evaluated on a 2,500-word vocabulary. This established that the articulatory musculature of the face and tongue remains informative after laryngectomy, and it showed that the algorithm could be trained without ever hearing the speaker's voice.



Vojtech and colleagues at Delsys asked whether the same EMG signal could also capture prosody. Working with four typical speakers and four laryngectomees, they used eight sEMG sensors to recognize lexical content and to classify whether the first word, last word, or no word in a phrase received stress. Across phrases drawn from a balanced eight-word stress-position corpus, they reported 96.3 percent word accuracy and 91.2 percent stress-location accuracy. When recognized words and stress patterns were synthesized into personalized voices, listeners rated output as significantly more intelligible and acceptable than electrolarynx alternatives.

Moving away from the face

The Meltzner work, for all its accuracy, lived with a practical ceiling. Sensors on the face and neck require adhesives, conductive gels, and careful placement; users described the experience as visually conspicuous and skin-irritating after long sessions. Newer work therefore tries to move sensing off the face into everyday wearable form factors.



Wu and colleagues at UC Berkeley demonstrated a wireless neckband holding ten dry, gold-plated, 3D-printed electrodes, paired with a coin-sized recording module behind the head. It classified an 11-word vocabulary of minimal pairs at 92.7 percent accuracy across two speakers. Ablations showed neck-only signals were nearly as informative as combined neck-and-face signals, and that neck EMG features could map to self-supervised acoustic representations from WavLM.



An ETH Zurich group led by Meier, Cossettini, and Benini built a textile neckband around the BioGAP-Ultra platform, integrating fourteen fully-dry, fully-differential channels into an adjustable fabric band. On an eight-word command set, they reported 87 percent accuracy for vocalized speech and 68 percent for silent articulation under five-fold cross-validation. Critically, they also tested leave-one-session-out performance after removing and repositioning the device between sessions, with accuracy dropping to 64 percent (vocalized) and 54 percent (silent). This quantifies the laboratory-to-daily-life gap.



A pilot study by Salomons and colleagues at the HiTZ Basque Center evaluated muscle placement directly for Spanish, ranking fourteen candidate muscles by per-channel phone-classification accuracy. They selected an asymmetric eight-channel configuration spanning five facial and three neck muscles, including placements not common in earlier literature, suggesting conventional layouts may miss useful signal.

The shift from custom decoders to language priors

Historically, SSR was framed as training a bespoke decoder per speaker and hoping it generalizes. Recent papers suggest a different framing: leverage frozen language models and train only a small physiological adapter.



Mohapatra and colleagues at Northwestern tested whether a frozen Llama2/Llama3 model could read unvoiced EMG through a small adapter projecting EMG features into the LLM embedding space. With six minutes of unvoiced training data and no audible reference signal, they reported a 0.49 word error rate on a closed-vocabulary EMG-to-text task, outperforming a specialized transducer baseline by twenty percent on the same vocabulary.



Satterlee and colleagues at San Diego State combined one under-chin EMG channel with one above-ear EEG channel in a low-power wearable setup. A Siamese few-shot segmenter, sensor fusion, and trigram language-model correction reached 95.25 percent sentence-level accuracy on a small military-command corpus.



Tang and colleagues at Cambridge and Beihang targeted stroke patients with dysarthria using a wearable two-channel textile throat choker with screen-printed graphene strain sensors. Their pipeline used 144-ms token segmentation, a distilled 1D ResNet-18 classifier, and two GPT-4o-mini agents for word reconstruction and fluent sentence expansion. Across five patients on a 47-word vocabulary assembled into 20 sentences, they reported 4.2 percent word error rate and 2.9 percent sentence error rate.

The commercial frontier and recent reviews

The most visible project in this space is AlterEgo, originally published by Arnav Kapur and collaborators at the MIT Media Lab in 2018. The original prototype used seven EMG channels across the lower face and neck, MFCC-style features, and a 1D CNN classifier. On a 10-digit vocabulary, it reached 92 percent median word accuracy across ten users with around 0.43 seconds latency from utterance completion to transcription.



Recent review papers converge on one conclusion: cross-session and cross-user robustness, not peak in-lab scores, is now the decisive bottleneck. The field has made clear progress in sensors, low-power hardware, and model quality, but day-to-day consistency under real wear remains the primary deployment challenge.

Where this leaves us

Three threads dominate current SSR progress: hardware migration toward dry wearable sensors, model migration toward pretrained language priors, and evaluation migration toward realistic cross-session testing. The accumulated evidence suggests Sub-vocal Recognition is not blocked by a single unsolved problem. Signals are measurable, algorithms are viable, and hardware form factors are approaching daily-wear practicality. The open challenge is integration: selecting modality, placement, priors, and calibration strategies that generalize across people, days, and contexts. Amtavla's work is positioned around solving that integration problem.
